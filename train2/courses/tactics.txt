## CHAPTER loose | Free pieces | theme=hanging
> A free piece is one that nobody defends. Taking one costs you nothing, and losing one costs you the game. This chapter trains one habit: after every move, look for pieces that nothing defends, his and yours.

### LESSON loose | Free pieces
goal: Spot a piece nobody defends, yours or his.
rule: After every move, ask: which pieces are undefended now, his and mine?
> In your games you left a piece hanging 49 times. Another 33 times you did not take a piece he left for you. Both mistakes have the same cure. After each move, his or yours, scan the board for pieces that nothing defends. It takes a few seconds, and it is the most valuable habit in chess.

## CHAPTER count | Count before you capture | theme=removal
> When a piece is defended, taking it starts a string of captures on one square. Count the attackers and the defenders before you start, and you know who comes out ahead.

### LESSON count | Count before you capture
goal: Work out who wins a string of captures on one square.
rule: Count attackers and defenders; start with the cheapest piece, and remember that a trade can remove a defender.
> Thirty-four of your mistakes lost material in a trade. A piece with more attackers than defenders can be won, but only if you capture in the right order: cheapest piece first. And a defender is not always a real defender: if it can be traded off, or cannot take back safely, do not count it.

## CHAPTER mate1 | Checkmate in one | theme=mate1
> Most mates in real games use one of a few patterns. Learn to recognise them, and look at every check before any other move.

### LESSON mate1 | Checkmate patterns in one
goal: Recognise the everyday mates: queen next to the king, two rooks, the knight in the corner, and pieces working together.
rule: Look at every check first: for each one, count the squares his king can still reach.
> A mate needs three things: a check, no square for the king, and no way to take or block the checking piece. The quickest way to find one is a fixed habit. Before any other move, look at every check you have. For each check, ask where his king can go. If the answer is nowhere, it is mate.

## CHAPTER backrank | The back rank | theme=backrank
> A king behind a wall of its own pawns is safe from the front but helpless from behind. Use this against him, and make sure he cannot use it against you.

### LESSON backrank | The back rank
goal: Use, and avoid, the king stuck behind its own pawns.
rule: If his king has no escape square, every rook or queen check on the back rank is a threat.
> Look at the king and the three pawns in front of it. If none of them has moved, the king has no escape square. Then a rook or queen landing on the back rank with check is mate, unless something can block or take it. Eleven of your mistakes allowed a mate. Count who guards the back rank, for both kings.

## CHAPTER fork | Forks | theme=fork
> A fork is one move that attacks two things at once. He can save only one of them. Knights are the best forkers, but every piece can do it, even a pawn.

### LESSON fork-knight | Knight forks
goal: One knight move, two targets.
rule: Look for two valuable pieces a knight's jump apart, and a safe square that hits both.
> A knight attacks in a strange shape, so people forget about it. That is why forks cost you 35 mistakes in your games. The clue is simple: find two of his valuable pieces, the king, queen or rooks, that a knight on one square would attack together. Then check that the square is safe, or that the move comes with check.

## CHAPTER deflect | Lure and drag away | theme=deflection
> A defender can be dragged away from its post, and a king can be lured onto a bad square. Both work because a piece can only be in one place at a time.
